How many commands can an IoT platform push out to real hardware at once — and still guarantee that every single one of them arrives?

We put a number on it. A fleet of **100,000 MQTT gateways** fronting **500,000 devices**, every device receiving a remote command and reporting telemetry on a fixed cycle. Two checkpoints: one command per device every five minutes, then one command per device every minute.

At the second checkpoint the platform delivered **500,000 commands every minute — 27.5 million over the hour — with none lost, none expired, and a 99th-percentile round trip of 364 milliseconds** against a ten-second delivery budget.

This is the story of that test, and of what it takes to make command delivery both guaranteed and fast.

## The gap we set out to close

Look at how IoT platforms are usually benchmarked — including by us — and you will find telemetry. Messages per second in, data points per second persisted, devices connected. That is the right measurement for most deployments, and our [published performance benchmarks](/docs/reference/architecture/performance/) cover it: up to a million devices and 33,333 data points per second on a single cluster.

What that record does not cover is traffic in the **other** direction: the platform reaching back out to the hardware, at scale, with a delivery guarantee attached.

That matters because the two failure modes are not comparable. A dropped telemetry message is a data-quality problem — a gap in a chart, a slightly less accurate average. A dropped **command** is an operational one: a curtailment that never happened, a valve that stayed open, a firmware rollout that silently skipped nine hundred devices. Systems that act on the physical world need the second guarantee, not just the first.

There was a second gap too. Our public benchmarks exercise the [MQTT Device API](/docs/reference/mqtt-api/getting-connected/), where each device holds its own connection. This test deliberately used the [MQTT Gateway API](/docs/reference/gateway-api/overview/) instead, where one gateway session multiplexes many devices behind it. That is how industrial and energy fleets are actually built, and it puts a very different shape of load on the platform: far fewer connections, far more per-device state behind each one.

## A fast-moving startup, and a requirement that could not slip

The team that brought us this problem was a startup growing quickly on the [ThingsBoard Gateway API](/docs/reference/gateway-api/overview/), with a fleet built at a **1:5 ratio** — one gateway for every five devices behind it. 100,000 gateways. 500,000 devices.

Their requirement was not throughput for its own sake. Every device had to be reachable with a command, on a fixed cycle, and every command had to be accounted for — delivered, or explicitly reported as failed. Never quietly lost. And the round trip had to stay fast enough that an operator issuing a fleet-wide action sees it complete, not sees it queue.

So we set two checkpoints against the same fleet, changing only the cycle:

| | Checkpoint 1 | Checkpoint 2 |
|---|---|---|
| gateways / devices | 100,000 / 500,000 | 100,000 / 500,000 |
| connections | persistent MQTTS | persistent MQTTS |
| per device, per cycle | 1 telemetry message + 1 two-way command | 1 telemetry message + 1 two-way command |
| cycle | every 5 minutes | **every minute** |
| commands per minute | 100,000 | **500,000** |
| duration | 1 hour | 1 hour |

Both ran on a genuine **three availability-zone deployment** — every tier spread across zones, Kafka with a replication factor of three and one broker per zone — because a delivery guarantee that only holds while all three zones are healthy is not much of a guarantee.

Telemetry from all 500,000 devices was ingested through the Gateway API and forwarded to Kafka for downstream consumption, one of the standard ThingsBoard integration patterns. Time-series storage throughput is already well covered by the published benchmarks linked above, so it was deliberately left outside this exercise: every millisecond of latency reported below belongs to the command path, not to a database write on the telemetry side.

## What "guaranteed" actually means

The commands in this test were **persistent RPC**, and that word carries specific mechanics rather than good intentions.

When a command is issued, ThingsBoard writes it to PostgreSQL before it goes anywhere. The command then moves through a lifecycle — queued, sent, delivered, and finally successful or expired — and each transition is persisted. Delivery to the gateway uses **MQTT QoS 1** over the [Gateway API RPC topics](/docs/reference/gateway-api/rpc/), so the broker keeps re-sending until the gateway acknowledges receipt.

The consequence is the part that matters: a command that has been accepted cannot simply evaporate. It exists as a row with a status, and something has to move it to a terminal state. If the gateway is briefly offline, the command waits. If it cannot be delivered before its expiration, it is recorded as expired rather than forgotten.

That guarantee also has to survive the platform restarting underneath it. A ThingsBoard core node holds the actor state for each device, and during a rolling update those actors migrate to other nodes. The transport-level identity of every in-flight command is persisted alongside it, so when an actor is recreated elsewhere it rebuilds the set of commands still awaiting a reply — and a reply that arrives after the move is still matched to its request.

## Built for volume before the volume arrived

There is one platform change worth calling out, because it is the difference between a guarantee that works and a guarantee that works fast.

When persistent RPC was first built, it was designed for what remote commands were then: occasional and high-value, issued one at a time — a firmware trigger, a setpoint change, a reboot. Writing each command to the database synchronously was the right trade for that shape. Durability mattered; volume did not.

A fleet that commands every device every minute is a different shape entirely, and a synchronous write per command is the first thing to give way under it. So we moved RPC persistence onto a **batched, asynchronous write path** — commands and their status transitions are grouped and written off the message-processing threads, while still preserving per-command ordering so a command's status can never be written before the command itself.

That change was in place before the first of these two checkpoints ran. It is also why guaranteed delivery and low latency stopped being a trade-off: nothing on the command path waits for a database commit.

## Checkpoint one: a command every five minutes

The first checkpoint passed on the first attempt, with the full fleet and the full hour.

**5,500,000 commands delivered. Zero expired. Zero failed.** Average round trip 200–233 ms, 99th percentile 330–376 ms. No queue recorded a timeout or a failure, consumer lag stayed bounded and drained to zero on every cycle, and not one telemetry publish failed.

100,000 gateway sessions were established and held for the entire run, spread across six MQTT transport instances to within **±1.1%** of an even split.

## Checkpoint two: a command every minute

Then we multiplied the message rate by five and ran it again.

**27,496,000 commands delivered. Zero expired. Zero failed. Zero redelivered.**

The striking part is not the total. It is the latency: average 203–254 ms, 99th percentile **317–364 ms** — statistically level with the five-minute checkpoint, at five times the load, and more than **27× inside** the ten-second budget.

100,000 sessions again, flat for the hour, this time within ±1.3% across the six transport instances.

And the command path itself stayed clean under the whole run — no failures, no timeouts, on any queue:

#### Where the load does show

Being honest about a benchmark means pointing at the place it is tightest, not only the place it looks best.

Commands in this test were dispatched as a **synchronized burst** — every device in the fleet commanded at the top of each cycle, rather than smoothly spread across it. That is the harder pattern, and it is the realistic one for fleet-wide actions like a coordinated curtailment or a mass configuration push.

In those burst windows the round trip rises to roughly one second on average, with a 99th percentile of 2.76 seconds. Still comfortably inside the ten-second budget — around 3.6× headroom rather than 27× — and that window is the number we would watch first if the workload grew. The steady-state figure is the level one; the burst figure is where five times the load becomes visible.

#### An efficiency result we did not expect

At the higher rate, Kafka moved **235,000 messages per second** — 3.4× the volume of the first checkpoint — while using slightly *less* CPU.

The reason is that Kafka's cost is dominated by request handling rather than message count. At the higher rate each produce request carried 3.4× more messages within the same batching window, and consumers received fuller batches per poll. The result: 3.4× the messages moved with **21% fewer requests**. Message throughput, in other words, is not what makes a Kafka tier expensive.

## What the second checkpoint took

The first checkpoint passed unchanged. The second took three attempts, and each failure pointed at something worth knowing for any high-volume deployment.

#### Storage throughput, not storage capacity

One attempt reached the full target rate, held it for about ten minutes, and then collapsed. Write throughput had saturated the volume's limit, write latency went from 23 ms to 171 ms, and the disk queue depth went from 9 to 50.

The cause was a tier boundary rather than a sizing mistake. On AWS, a PostgreSQL `gp3` volume gives no throughput control below 400 GiB — below that it is fixed at 3,000 IOPS and 125 MB/s, no matter what you ask for. Growing the volume to 400 GiB raised the included baseline to **12,000 IOPS and 500 MB/s** and the ceiling disappeared.

Worth stressing: capacity was never the issue. A one-hour run at this rate consumes about 19 GiB, so the original 100 GiB was ample on space and hopeless on throughput.

![Database write latency across the run](/images/blog/commanding-the-fleet-how-we-delivered-500k-guaranteed-commands-per-minute/rds-write-latency.png)

#### Write amplification on a status column

With the storage ceiling lifted, the next attempt still failed — at only 28% of the new throughput ceiling, so storage was demonstrably no longer the constraint.

The cause was subtler. PostgreSQL can often update a row in place without touching its indexes, which keeps the write cost of a status change small. But it treats a column named in a **partial index predicate** as an indexed column — and there was such an index whose predicate referenced the command's `status`.

A command's lifecycle is nothing but a sequence of status transitions. So every transition was disqualified from the cheap in-place path, and each one wrote a new row version plus entries in every index on the table. Removing that index brought in-place updates back to 52.5% of writes, halving the garbage generated.

#### Cleanup that could keep up

Even then, dead row versions accumulated, because PostgreSQL's autovacuum was running with its default rate limit — roughly 32 MB/s. Each pass reclaimed about 1.7 million dead rows while 3.1 million more arrived.

Lifting the rate limit dropped a cleanup pass from 227 seconds to about 50, and dead rows fell from 33.9% of the table to 0.9% within three minutes. The run finished with 740 dead rows — 0.0% of the table.

Both of these are deployment-level settings rather than platform behaviour, and both are worth applying deliberately in any deployment where high-volume persistent RPC is the primary workload.

## What it runs on

One of the advantages of the ThingsBoard microservice architecture is that you scale the tier under pressure, not the whole cluster. The fleet above ran on a deliberately modest footprint:

- **6 MQTT Transport instances** — 100,000 persistent gateway sessions, TLS, and the telemetry stream.
- **6 Core instances** — device state, session routing, and the command lifecycle.
- **3 Rule Engine instances** — command dispatch and telemetry routing.
- **3 Kafka brokers**, replication factor 3, one per availability zone.
- **6 × `r8g.xlarge` compute nodes** (4 vCPU / 32 GiB) plus 2 small monitoring nodes.
- **One `db.m7g.xlarge` Multi-AZ PostgreSQL instance**, 400 GiB `gp3`.

That is four vCPU per node carrying 500,000 devices and half a million commands a minute.

The reason it is four and not eight is a measurement rather than a guess. The first configuration used eight-vCPU nodes and showed the cluster **memory-bound with CPU nearly idle** — cores at 1–7% while heap sat near its limit. So we halved the vCPU while holding memory constant, and peak node CPU during the validated run was 69%, with no measured loss of performance.

![JVM heap by tier — the tightest resource in the cluster](/images/blog/commanding-the-fleet-how-we-delivered-500k-guaranteed-commands-per-minute/heap-by-tier.png)

![Node CPU and memory during the run](/images/blog/commanding-the-fleet-how-we-delivered-500k-guaranteed-commands-per-minute/node-cpu-memory.png)

**The practical lesson: size this workload on heap first.** Across both checkpoints, JVM heap was the tightest resource in every tier — transport peaking at 84.5% — while CPU had room to spare everywhere. Garbage collection consumed under 1% of wall time, so heap was not under pressure; it was simply the dimension with the least headroom, and the one to grow first as a fleet expands.

## What this means for your fleet

Command and control has been the quiet half of IoT platform benchmarking. It is also the half that carries operational consequences: the fleet-wide firmware push, the demand-response signal, the emergency shutdown. Those are the moments where "we processed 99.9% of messages" is not an acceptable answer.

What these two checkpoints establish is that guaranteed delivery and low latency are not a trade-off on ThingsBoard. Half a million commands a minute, to half a million devices behind 100,000 gateways, across three availability zones — every one persisted, every one accounted for, and a 99th-percentile round trip well under half a second.

They also establish something about cost shape. This ran on six four-vCPU nodes and a single database instance. Scaling command volume did not require scaling the cluster; it required knowing which two or three settings actually bind.
