How many commands can an IoT platform push out to real hardware at once — and still guarantee that every single one of them arrives?

A startup preparing to launch on the **ThingsBoard MQTT Gateway API** asked us exactly that, and gave us the shape of the answer: a baseline fleet of **100,000 gateways and 500,000 devices**, held at two command rates — one command per device every five minutes, then one every minute.

So we put a number on it. At the faster rate the platform delivered **500,000 commands every minute — 27.5 million over the hour — with none lost, none expired, and 99% of commands reaching their gateway inside 400 ms**.

A story of measurement over assumption — and proof that data collection is only half of what ThingsBoard does well.

## Why commands need their own benchmark

Ask how an IoT platform performs and the answer comes back in one of two forms: telemetry throughput, or the number of connections it can hold at once. That is the right measurement for most deployments, and our [published performance benchmarks](/docs/reference/architecture/performance/) cover it in depth.

Those numbers measure ingestion. They say nothing about how the platform performs when it has to send remote commands back to the hardware.

The two are not the same, because the failure modes are not comparable. Most telemetry pipelines are built to tolerate the occasional lost message, and for good reason — one missing reading is a gap in a chart, a slightly less accurate average, and the next reading is already on its way. A dropped command has no equivalent. It is a machine that never stopped, a valve that stayed open, a firmware rollout that silently skipped part of the fleet. That is why command delivery needs a guarantee telemetry can live without.

## The challenge

The challenge was not just measuring command delivery alongside telemetry ingestion. Three things made it harder than a throughput run.

**Four database writes per command.** Less a challenge than a bottleneck, but it set the ceiling. A persistent command is a row that moves through QUEUED, SENT, DELIVERED and SUCCESSFUL — one insert and three updates. At half a million commands a minute that is **two million row writes a minute**, and originally every one of them was committed synchronously on the thread handling the command.

**Sizing for the fleet, not just for the message rate.** A gateway is a device too, so this fleet is **600,000 actors** in the core service — 100,000 gateways and the 500,000 devices behind them — and they are not created for free: each one is created inside its tenant's actor, one at a time. A steady-state message rate tells you nothing about this. It is the part of scale that shows up when the fleet comes *up* rather than while it is running — at first boot, and again whenever the cluster is brought up from stopped.

**A deployment that can lose a zone.** Every tier spread across three availability zones, with Kafka replicating across all of them — no two brokers share a zone, so every message crosses a boundary. A latency figure from a single-zone cluster tells you nothing about a cluster that survives losing a zone, so the numbers below are measured on the topology a production fleet would actually run.

## Solution strategy

#### Taking the commit off the command path

[Persistent RPC](/docs/user-guide/command-and-control/) is what stands behind the guarantee: a command is a row before it is sent anywhere, and every step of its life is written too. A separate synchronous write per state was a fine trade when commands were occasional — a firmware trigger, a setpoint change, a reboot — which is what persistent RPC was built for.

So we changed it before running anything. Inserts and status updates are now grouped into **batches** and written off the message-processing threads: the same rows, the same durability, without a database commit standing in the path of every command.

#### Parallelising fleet start-up

Every device in ThingsBoard is an actor in the core service, holding its session routing and the set of commands in flight for it — and since a gateway is itself a device, this fleet is 600,000 actors rather than 500,000. That is why the workload is bound by memory rather than by CPU.

Two things parallelise their creation, and they compose. Devices are routed to core instances by device id, and **each core instance runs its own tenant actor**, so a single tenant's fleet already comes up in parallel across the cluster. Tenants multiply that again, because each tenant is a separate serialised stream on every instance. Six core instances across five tenants is thirty concurrent creation streams instead of one.

![Device-actor creation across six core instances and five tenants — thirty independent streams](/images/blog/commanding-the-fleet-how-we-delivered-500k-guaranteed-commands-per-minute/actor-creation-parallelism.svg)

That is why the core tier is six instances and the fleet is split five ways — both chosen for start-up, not for steady state. The cost lands on a cold start: a first boot, or a cluster brought back up after being stopped. A rolling restart is far cheaper — partitions migrate to the surviving instances, where the cache is already warm and the per-device lookup is negligible. In these runs the full fleet of 100,000 gateways and 500,000 devices was connected, announced and ready in about five minutes.

#### An architecture that can lose a zone

One advantage of the ThingsBoard microservice architecture is that you scale the tier under pressure, not the whole cluster. The fleet ran on a deliberately modest footprint, every tier spread across three availability zones:

- **6 MQTT Transport instances** — 100,000 gateway sessions, TLS, and the telemetry stream
- **6 Core instances** — device state, session routing, and the command lifecycle
- **3 Rule Engine instances** — command dispatch and telemetry routing
- **3 Kafka brokers**, replication factor 3, one per availability zone
- **6 Valkey nodes** (3 primary, 3 replica) — the session and credential cache, with a primary and its replica never in the same zone
- **6 × `r8g.xlarge` nodes** (4 vCPU / 32 GiB) plus 2 small monitoring nodes
- **1 × `db.m7g.xlarge` Multi-AZ PostgreSQL instance**, 100 GiB `gp3`

We did not start from a blank sheet. Our standard Private Cloud sizing is tested to 100,000 devices; this fleet is 100,000 *connections* with 600,000 device actors behind them, so the node class was the first thing to re-derive.

Measurement, not estimate, decided it. On eight-vCPU nodes the cluster was **memory-bound with CPU nearly idle** — cores at 1–7% while heap sat near 90% of its limit — so vCPU was halved while memory was held constant. Peak node CPU stayed under 76%, with no measured loss of performance, and heap remained the tightest resource in every tier. This is a workload to size on memory first.

![JVM heap by tier — the tightest resource in the cluster](/images/blog/commanding-the-fleet-how-we-delivered-500k-guaranteed-commands-per-minute/heap-by-tier.png)

![Node CPU and memory during the run](/images/blog/commanding-the-fleet-how-we-delivered-500k-guaranteed-commands-per-minute/node-cpu-memory.png)

**What we tuned.** Nothing here is exotic, but none of it is default either. These are the settings a deployment carrying this shape of load should decide rather than inherit.

| setting | default | ours | why |
|---|---|---|---|
| `transport.sessions.report_timeout` | 3 s | 90 s | every live session is checked at the end of each period |
| `actors.system.device_dispatcher_pool_size` | 4 | 10 | device actors carry the command lifecycle, and there are 500,000 of them |
| `actors.system.rule_dispatcher_pool_size` | 8 | 32 | every command is dispatched through the rule engine |
| `actors.system.tenant_dispatcher_pool_size` | 2 | 5 | one thread per tenant |
| `queue.core.partitions` | 10 | 12 | two partitions per core instance |
| `spring.datasource.maximumPoolSize` | 16 | 72 | batch writers each hold a connection while flushing |
| Kafka producer | — | lz4, `acks=1`, `linger.ms=5`, 64 KiB batches | fewer and fuller requests, rather than fewer messages |

The first one is worth explaining. Session activity is reported on a fixed period rather than per message, and at the end of every period the transport examines **every** live session, not only the ones that were active. Those states are held in memory, and at 100,000 sessions the three-second default means each transport instance walks every session it holds, twenty times a minute. Raising the period to 90 seconds cuts that by thirty times, and coalesces several activities from one device into a single update.

## The benchmark, and what it showed

The fleet connects through the [MQTT Gateway API](/docs/reference/gateway-api/overview/) rather than the [Device API](/docs/reference/mqtt-api/getting-connected/) our published figures use — one gateway session carrying many devices behind it, the way industrial fleets are actually built. It is a very different shape of load: far fewer connections, far more per-device state behind each one.

Telemetry from all 500,000 devices was ingested through the Gateway API and forwarded to Kafka for downstream consumption, one of the standard ThingsBoard integration patterns. Time-series storage throughput is already well covered by the published benchmarks linked above, so it was deliberately left outside this exercise.

So we set two checkpoints against the same fleet, changing only the cycle:

| | Checkpoint 1 | Checkpoint 2 |
|---|---|---|
| gateways / devices | 100,000 / 500,000 | 100,000 / 500,000 |
| tenants | 5, each with 20,000 gateways / 100,000 devices | 5, each with 20,000 gateways / 100,000 devices |
| connections | 100,000, held open for the full run | 100,000, held open for the full run |
| connection mode | One-way TLS | One-way TLS |
| per device, per cycle | 1 telemetry message + 1 two-way command | 1 telemetry message + 1 two-way command |
| telemetry delivery | MQTT QoS 0 | MQTT QoS 0 |
| command delivery | MQTT QoS 1, persistent RPC | MQTT QoS 1, persistent RPC |
| cycle | every 5 minutes | **every minute** |
| commands per minute | 100,000 | **500,000** |
| duration | 1 hour | 1 hour |

Both passed, with the full fleet and the full hour.

- **5,500,000 commands** delivered at the five-minute cadence and **27.5 million** at one minute — none lost, none expired.
- **Delivery time did not move.** Average 200–233 ms at the slower cadence against 203–254 ms at the faster one; 99th percentile 330–376 ms against 317–364 ms. Five times the load, the same latency.
- **100,000 sessions held for the entire hour**, spread across the six transport instances to within ±1.1% and ±1.3% of an even split.
- **No queue recorded a timeout or a failure**, consumer lag stayed bounded and drained to zero on every cycle, and not one telemetry publish failed.

![Command queue outcomes — no failures, no timeouts](/images/blog/commanding-the-fleet-how-we-delivered-500k-guaranteed-commands-per-minute/command-queue-outcomes.png)

## What the faster rate took

The slower cadence passed on the configuration above, unchanged. Five times the rate demanded two more things.

**Storage throughput, not capacity.** The five-minute cadence ran on a 100 GiB `gp3` volume at its default 125 MB/s and never came close to the ceiling. At five times the rate it binds — and a PostgreSQL `gp3` volume gives no throughput control below 400 GiB, so the only way up is to grow the volume. At 400 GiB the included baseline becomes **12,000 IOPS and 500 MB/s**. Capacity was never the reason to do it: an hour at this rate consumes about 19 GiB.

The dashed line on the chart below is that default ceiling. Steady state sits at about 70% of it, and the peak goes 1.6× over — on the default tier, both would have been throttled.

![Database write throughput against the 125 MB/s default gp3 ceiling](/images/blog/commanding-the-fleet-how-we-delivered-500k-guaranteed-commands-per-minute/rds-write-throughput.png)

**Cleanup that keeps up.** Every status change rewrites a row, and PostgreSQL leaves the old version behind for autovacuum to reclaim. Watching the database during the run, we could see the table gaining dead rows faster than cleanup was clearing them — each pass reclaimed about 1.7 million while 3.1 million more arrived. Left alone, that trend ends in a table that is mostly garbage, with latency climbing as the database works through it.

The limit was a rate cap rather than a shortage of capacity: autovacuum ships with a cost delay that holds it to roughly 32 MB/s. We lifted it while the run was still going, and a cleanup pass went from 227 seconds to about 50. Dead rows fell from 6.1 million to 147,000 — 33.9% of the table to under 1% — in under four minutes.

The catch-up itself is the spike in the middle of the chart below: clearing a backlog that size costs write throughput while it runs. What matters is where it lands afterwards.

![Database write latency, annotated at the moment the autovacuum rate cap was lifted](/images/blog/commanding-the-fleet-how-we-delivered-500k-guaranteed-commands-per-minute/rds-write-latency.png)

Database CPU makes the same point from a different angle. It had been climbing for twenty minutes **at a constant command rate** — the signature of work accumulating rather than load increasing — and after the change it flattened and stayed flat for the rest of the run.

![Database CPU utilisation, annotated at the same moment](/images/blog/commanding-the-fleet-how-we-delivered-500k-guaranteed-commands-per-minute/rds-cpu-utilization.png)

Both are deployment settings rather than platform behaviour, and both are worth deciding deliberately wherever high-volume persistent RPC is the main workload.

## The takeaway

Command and control has been the quiet half of IoT platform benchmarking. It is also the half that carries operational consequences: the fleet-wide firmware push, the demand-response signal, the emergency shutdown. Those are the moments where "we processed 99.9% of messages" is not an acceptable answer.

What these two checkpoints establish is that guaranteed delivery and low latency are not a trade-off on ThingsBoard. Half a million commands a minute, to half a million devices behind 100,000 gateways, across three availability zones — every one persisted, every one accounted for, and a 99th-percentile delivery time well under half a second. On six four-vCPU nodes and a single database instance.
