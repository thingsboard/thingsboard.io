How many commands can an IoT platform push out to real hardware at once — and still guarantee that every single one of them arrives?

An enterprise partner preparing to launch on the [MQTT Gateway API](/docs/reference/gateway-api/overview/) asked us exactly that, and gave us the shape of the answer: a baseline fleet of 100,000 gateways and 500,000 devices, five behind every gateway, at two command rates — one command per device every five minutes, then one every minute.

So we put a number on it. At the faster rate the platform delivered **500,000 commands every minute**, **27.5 million over the hour**, with none lost and none expired. 99% of them reached their gateway inside 400 ms, including the cross-zone traffic between services.

A story of measurement over assumption — and proof that data collection is only half of what ThingsBoard does well.

## Why commands need their own benchmark

Ask how an IoT platform performs and the answer comes back in one of two forms: telemetry throughput, or the number of connections it can hold at once. That is the right measurement for most deployments, and our [published performance benchmarks](/docs/reference/architecture/performance/) cover it in depth, measured over the [Device API](/docs/reference/mqtt-api/getting-connected/) with one connection per device.

Those numbers measure ingestion. They say nothing about how the platform performs when it has to send remote commands back to the hardware.

The two are not the same, because the failure modes are not comparable. Most telemetry pipelines are built to tolerate the occasional lost message, and for good reason — one missing reading is a gap in a chart, a slightly less accurate average, and the next reading is already on its way. A dropped command has no equivalent. It is a machine that never stopped, a valve that stayed open, a firmware rollout that silently skipped part of the fleet. That is why command delivery needs a guarantee telemetry can live without.

## The challenge

The challenge was not just measuring command delivery alongside telemetry ingestion. Four things made it harder than a throughput run.

**Commands arrive in batches.** At the partner's request one call names 500 devices instead of 500 separate calls, to keep HTTP traffic down. The whole fleet is commanded on a cycle, so a handful of calls a second expand into thousands of individually tracked commands.

**Four database writes per command.** Less a challenge than a bottleneck, but it set the ceiling. A [persistent RPC](/docs/user-guide/command-and-control/) delivered to a gateway subscribed at MQTT QoS 1 is a row that moves through QUEUED, SENT, DELIVERED and SUCCESSFUL — one insert and three updates. At half a million commands a minute that is **two million row writes a minute**, and originally every one of them was committed synchronously on the core service's device-actor thread, which carries all the other work of those devices too and sits idle until the commit returns. That is a ceiling no setting can raise — the wait itself has to come off the command path.

**Onboarding the whole fleet, not just carrying its message rate.** A gateway is a device too, so this fleet is **600,000 actors** in the core service — 100,000 gateways and the 500,000 devices behind them — and they are not created for free. Each one is created inside its tenant's actor, one at a time. A steady-state message rate tells you nothing about how long that takes. The requirement was that the fleet comes online quickly — from a cold cluster, after a full restart, and as new sets of gateways and their devices are added.

**Measuring under real cross-zone latency.** ThingsBoard services talk to each other through Kafka: transport, core and rule engine each hand the message on, and a command makes several of those hops on its way to a gateway. Spread across three availability zones the way a production fleet is deployed, one broker per zone, most of those hops cross a zone boundary and pay for it. Delivery latency had to be measured with that cost present, because a single-zone figure does not carry over to the cluster anyone would really run.

## Solution strategy

#### Turning one REST call into 500 commands

The batch lands on the queue carrying inbound API calls, and the 500 commands it becomes go onto the queue carrying commands. Both are separate from the telemetry queue, so commands and ingestion never block each other. Every queue is partitioned to divide evenly across the three rule-engine instances.

![One batch call fanning out through the REST API calls queue into 500 individual commands on the RPC calls queue](/images/blog/500k-guaranteed-iot-device-commands-per-minute/rpc-queue-split.svg)

#### Getting the database writes off the command processing path

Persistent RPC is what stands behind the guarantee: a command is a row before it is sent anywhere, and every step of its life is written too. A separate synchronous write per state was a fine trade when commands were occasional — a firmware trigger, a setpoint change, a reboot — which is what persistent RPC was built for.

So we changed it. Inserts and status updates are grouped into **batches** and written by a dedicated pool of database writer threads. The same rows and the same durability, but the device actor hands its write off and moves on to the next message instead of waiting for a database commit. A command still goes out only once the batch reports its row as durable, so nothing is ever sent that was not written down first — the wait is a callback now rather than a blocked thread.

#### Parallelising fleet onboarding

Every device in ThingsBoard is an actor in the core service, holding its session routing and the set of commands in flight for it. That is what makes this workload bound by memory rather than by CPU.

A device actor is created the first time its gateway connects or announces it, and inside a tenant's actor those creations happen strictly one at a time — each device has to be looked up before its actor can exist. The first lookup goes to PostgreSQL. From then on the device is in the Valkey cache, and the next one costs a fraction of that.

![Device-actor creation inside one tenant actor — mailbox, create, ready, then the next device](/images/blog/500k-guaranteed-iot-device-commands-per-minute/actor-creation-bottleneck.svg)

So a single stream cannot be made faster. It can be multiplied, and two things multiply it. Devices are routed to core instances by device id, and **each core instance runs its own tenant actor**, so one tenant's fleet already comes online in parallel across the cluster. Tenants multiply that again, because each tenant is a separate stream on every instance.

![Devices routed by hash to six core instances, each running five tenant actors — thirty onboarding streams at once](/images/blog/500k-guaranteed-iot-device-commands-per-minute/actor-creation-streams.svg)

Onboarding speed is therefore something you can design, not a fixed property of the platform. Add core instances, or split the fleet across more tenants, and the number of streams rises with it. The same arithmetic sizes a fleet several times larger.

Only one of the two was ours to set. The five-way split came with the fleet as the agreed baseline, and the six core instances came from the cluster sizing in the next section. What the fast-onboarding requirement changed is that their product had to be verified rather than assumed. In these runs the full fleet was connected, announced and ready in about five minutes.

That cost is paid once, on a cold start. In a rolling restart the partitions move to instances that already have those devices cached, so the lookups are cheap and the fleet comes back much faster.

#### The starting point: a real three-zone cluster

Our standard ThingsBoard Private Cloud environments are tuned and tested to support up to 100,000 devices, on six MQTT transport instances with three monolith ThingsBoard nodes behind them. This fleet holds the same number of connections, but every one of them is a gateway, so 600,000 device actors sit behind them.

So the core and the rule engine were split into separate services, which is what lets either of them be scaled, restarted or sized without touching the other. The core tier was scaled out from three instances to **six**, both to hold those actors and to onboard them quickly. The rule engine stayed at three.

Every tier is then spread across three availability zones, so losing a zone costs a share of capacity rather than the cluster. The price shows up in latency, because a command's Kafka hops now cross zone boundaries, and that cost sits inside all the figures below.

![Three availability zones, two nodes each running one MQTT transport, one core and one Valkey pod, with a rule engine and a Kafka broker per zone, in front of a Multi-AZ PostgreSQL instance](/images/blog/500k-guaranteed-iot-device-commands-per-minute/cluster-three-zone-topology.svg)

Sizing it all to divide evenly is the principle worth carrying away, and partitions are where that is easiest to miss. The cluster's partitioned topics belong to two families. Two of them feed the core tier: the requests transports send to core, and the traffic that drives the device actors. The others are the rule engine's queues, partitioned per queue — the command queue from earlier among them, which is where a burst has to spread. Twelve partitions divides cleanly into both tiers, two per core instance and four per rule-engine instance, so no instance is handed a heavier share than its neighbour. Scale a tier and its topics have to be repartitioned with it.

## The first run: one command per device every five minutes

The first run used the cluster described above, at the slower of the two command rates. Every five minutes each of the 500,000 devices sent one telemetry message and received one two-way command, a command it had to answer rather than merely receive. That works out to 500,000 telemetry messages and 500,000 commands a cycle, or **100,000 commands a minute**, sustained for an hour. The telemetry went through the Gateway API to the rule engine, which forwarded it to Kafka for the partner's own downstream systems, one of the standard ThingsBoard integration patterns.

| | |
|---|---|
| fleet | 100,000 gateways and 500,000 devices, five behind every gateway |
| tenants | 5, each with 20,000 gateways and 100,000 devices |
| connections | 100,000, one-way TLS, open for the full run |
| dispatch | batches of 500 devices per call, about 3 calls a second |
| per device, per cycle | 1 telemetry message and 1 two-way command |
| telemetry delivery | MQTT QoS 0, 21 metric keys per message |
| command delivery | MQTT QoS 1, persistent RPC |
| cycle | every 5 minutes |
| duration | 1 hour |

Here is what the hour produced.

- **5,500,000 commands over the hour**, none lost and none expired.
- **Delivery time averaged 200–233 ms**, with a 99th percentile of 330–376 ms.
- **100,000 connections stayed up for the entire hour**, spread across the six transport instances to within ±1.1% of an even split.
- **35,000 telemetry data points a second** ingested alongside the commands, with not one publish failure.
- **No queue recorded a timeout or a failure**, and consumer lag stayed bounded, draining to zero on every cycle.
- **The cluster was not working hard.** Node CPU peaked at 67%, and heap was the tightest resource anywhere at 82% of its limit in the transport tier.

## The second run: five times the rate

The first run finished with room to spare, and the tightest resource in it, heap in the transport tier, is driven mostly by the 100,000 connections rather than by how often commands are sent. Sending them five times more often should not move it much, so we ran the second one on the same infrastructure and changed only the cycle, from five minutes to one. Every other figure below follows from that one change. The point was to find what gave way first.

| | first run | second run |
|---|---|---|
| cycle | every 5 minutes | **every minute** |
| dispatch | ~3 calls a second | **~17 calls a second** |
| commands | 100,000 a minute | **500,000 a minute** |
| telemetry | ~35,000 data points a second | **~175,000 data points a second** |

**It did not hold.** The run reached the target rate and stayed there for about ten minutes. Then the core service started to report that the rows waiting to be written were growing faster than the writer threads could clear them.

```
Queue-N [RPC] is falling behind: queueSize [1711] exceeds batchSize [1000]
```

That message points in one direction, at the database, and the database confirmed it at once. Write latency grew from 23 ms to 171 ms, and the disk queue grew from 9 requests to 50. The disk was at its limit, but not on space and not on bandwidth. A 100 GiB `gp3` volume gives 125 MB/s and 3,000 IOPS, the number of write operations it can do each second. The IOPS were the limit we reached. Write throughput stopped at 96–98 MB/s and went no higher.

**A bigger volume, for the IOPS.** A `gp3` volume under 400 GiB lets you change neither number, so we grew it to 400 GiB for the experiment. That raises the baseline to **12,000 IOPS and 500 MB/s**, four times more of both.

We also had a second suspicion, because a persistent command is four writes rather than one. Each state change writes a new copy of the row and leaves the old copy for a background job, autovacuum, to remove, so at half a million commands a minute those old copies arrive very fast. The plan was to see whether the extra IOPS were enough on their own, and to watch the old copies while we did.

**Cleanup was too slow.** About half an hour into the next run the slowdown returned, and this time the commands were delayed by it. The old copies were the reason. Each pass cleaned about 1.7 million of them while 3.1 million more arrived. Autovacuum is rate-limited by default, to roughly 32 MB/s, so that it never competes with live work, and that is too slow for a table changing this fast. We changed two settings on it while the run was going. The pause between cleanup steps went from 2 ms to none, and the point where cleanup starts moved from 10% of the table to 4%.

```sql
ALTER TABLE rpc SET (autovacuum_vacuum_cost_delay = 0, autovacuum_vacuum_scale_factor = 0.04);
```

One cleanup pass went from 227 seconds to about 50, and the old copies fell from a third of the table to less than 1% in under four minutes. The chart below shows the whole shape of it. Write throughput had been climbing while the backlog grew, because each pass had more to clear than the one before. It peaked at 198 MB/s as the catch-up ran, 1.6 times above what the old volume allowed, and then settled at 87 MB/s for the rest of the run. Even that steady level is about 70% of the old 125 MB/s limit.

![Database write throughput against the 125 MB/s default gp3 ceiling](/images/blog/500k-guaranteed-iot-device-commands-per-minute/rds-write-throughput.png)

The write operations show it from the other side, and they are the number that had run out. With cleanup keeping up, the run settled at about 2,000 a second, two thirds of what the original 100 GiB volume allowed. The only thing that needed more was the catch-up, which touched 7,010.

![Database write IOPS against the 3,000 limit of a 100 GiB gp3 volume](/images/blog/500k-guaranteed-iot-device-commands-per-minute/rds-write-iops.png)

Write latency shows the change most plainly of all, from 171 ms at the worst of it to between 4 and 8 ms once cleanup was winning.

![Database write latency, annotated at the moment the autovacuum rate cap was lifted](/images/blog/500k-guaranteed-iot-device-commands-per-minute/rds-write-latency.png)

Database CPU tells the same story. It had been growing for twenty minutes while the command rate stayed the same, which means work was building up rather than load increasing. After the change it went flat and stayed flat until the end of the run.

![Database CPU utilisation, annotated at the same moment](/images/blog/500k-guaranteed-iot-device-commands-per-minute/rds-cpu-utilization.png)

**What it cost the commands.** The queue of commands waiting for the core service normally sits between 1,800 and 4,300 messages and drains on every cycle. While cleanup was losing the race it grew to 32,207, and then to 54,918, which at 8,333 commands a second is several seconds of extra waiting for everything behind it. Three minutes after the change it was back in its usual range, and it stayed there for the rest of the run. No command was lost in those minutes, because a persistent command waits in the queue rather than being dropped, but the delivery times in that window are the one part of the run that would not have met the target.

Here is what this hour of the run produced.

- **27.5 million commands over the hour**, none lost and none expired.
- **Delivery time did not move.** It averaged 203–254 ms against 200–233 ms in the first run, with a 99th percentile of 317–364 ms against 330–376 ms. Five times the load, the same latency.
- **100,000 connections stayed up for the entire hour** again, spread across the six transport instances to within ±1.3% of an even split.
- **175,000 telemetry data points a second** ingested alongside the commands, with not one publish failure.
- **The cluster was working harder, but not hard.** Node CPU peaked at 75%, and heap was again the tightest resource anywhere, at 84% of its limit in the transport tier.

Both are deployment settings rather than platform behaviour, and both are worth deciding deliberately wherever high-volume persistent RPC is the main workload.

## The takeaway

Command and control has been the quiet half of IoT platform benchmarking. It is also the half that carries operational consequences: the fleet-wide firmware push, the demand-response signal, the emergency shutdown. Those are the moments where "we processed 99.9% of messages" is not an acceptable answer.

What these two runs establish is that guaranteed delivery and low latency are not a trade-off on ThingsBoard. Half a million commands a minute, to half a million devices behind 100,000 gateways, across three availability zones — every one persisted, every one accounted for, and a 99th-percentile delivery time well under half a second. On six four-vCPU nodes and a single database instance.
